Origin Story — The Garland That Changed Everything

As a young boy, Manjunath was never initially fascinated with Karnatak music despite growing up in a deeply musical household. His father Vidwan Mahadevappa and elder brother Mysore Nagaraj were already established musicians. His strict father would take young Manjunath to all concerts.

At a pivotal moment — after a violin duet concert by his father and brother — the entire team on stage was garlanded except young Manjunath, who sat behind his father throughout the nearly 4-hour concert. The organiser promised he would be garlanded only if he performed the following year. That single incident ignited the amazing journey of Manjunath to becoming Dr. Mysore Manjunath.

His father and brother used to practice eight to ten hours a day under strict discipline: "If we didn't practice in the morning, there was no breakfast; if we didn't practice in the evening, there was no dinner."

Academic Distinction

Dr. Manjunath is one of the rare classical musicians in India who combines the highest level of performing artistry with rigorous academic scholarship — holding a Ph.D and four Gold Medals from the University of Mysore, having guided multiple research students as a distinguished Professor, and been honoured as the Cultural Ambassador of his university.

Guru-Shishya Classification

Dr. Manjunath received his entire foundational training directly under his father, Late Vidwan Prof. S. Mahadevappa — a legendary violinist of Mysore. This direct guru-shishya transmission preserves the authentic Mysore Bani (style) of Carnatic violin playing — a tradition spanning generations from the classical lineage of the Mysore royal court's musical culture.

Chapter Three

Gurukula Lineage & Musical Heritage

The Mysore School of Violin

The Mysore tradition of Carnatic violin is distinct from the Chennai/Madras tradition. Characterized by deep melodic phrasing, intricate gamakas (ornaments), restrained classical austerity, and adherence to raga grammar, the Mysore style traces its roots to the royal patronage of the Wodeyar dynasty's musical court.

Late Vidwan Prof. S. Mahadevappa was a pillar of this tradition in Mysore. Despite recommendations from legends like Dr. M. Balamuralikrishna and Dr. N. Ramani to relocate to Chennai (then considered essential for a musician's career), Mahadevappa insisted on remaining in Mysore — a decision that preserved and elevated Mysore as a living seat of classical music. Dr. Manjunath has continued and expanded this legacy.

The Mysore Brothers Duo

Dr. Mysore Manjunath and his elder brother Mysore Nagaraj are collectively celebrated as the "Mysore Brothers" — one of the most celebrated violin duos in Carnatic music. Both are sons and direct disciples of Vidwan Mahadevappa. They perform in the rare jugalbandi (duet) format, and their combined concerts, especially the signature piece "Krishna Nee Begane Baro", are known to move audiences to tears.

They were described as a "Dream Team" for bringing academic and technical brilliance together with virtuosity.

Generational Legacy Continues

Dr. Manjunath's son Sumanth Manjunath is already an accomplished violinist who received a Gold Medal for achieving the top rank in his Master's in Music programme at the University of Mysore — carrying forward three generations of musical excellence from a single family.

Chapter Thirteen

Impact on Society

Cultural Diplomacy & National Representation

Dr. Manjunath was handpicked by the Government of India as a Cultural Ambassador to represent the nation at its most sovereign moments — the Republic Day and Independence Day celebrations in Sri Lanka and Iran. His music served not merely as entertainment but as a diplomatic bridge, articulating India's cultural identity to the world in its most formal ceremonial context.

Pandemic Humanitarian Response — "Life Again" (2020)

During the COVID-19 pandemic, when the world was in its deepest crisis, Dr. Manjunath conceptualized and composed "Life Again" — inviting 20 musicians from coronavirus-affected countries (including a musician from Wuhan, China) to collaborate. The message was powerful: this is a time for social distancing, not emotional distancing. Launched by ICCR in over 150 countries, the project demonstrated how Indian classical music can heal, unite, and inspire across geopolitical boundaries.

Yoga & Spiritual Heritage — "Yoga Niyoga" (2019)

When India's most profound gift to global wellness — Yoga — was being reduced to a fitness routine, Dr. Manjunath, commissioned by the Centre for Soft Power, composed a Yoga Anthem that reconnected Yoga to its spiritual, philosophical roots. Released at the British Parliament on World Yoga Day, the anthem was a statement of civilizational pride. His brand-new Raga Bharata (named after India's legendary first emperor) gave the anthem not just musical identity but civilizational meaning.

Elevation of Mysuru as a Cultural Capital

By deliberately choosing to remain based in Mysuru despite offers to relocate to Chennai, Dr. Manjunath has, over decades, reinforced Mysuru's identity as a living seat of Carnatic classical music — completing what his father began. His international stature draws global attention to Mysuru, not just as a heritage tourist destination but as a city that continues to produce world-class classical artistry in the 21st century.

Education & Mentorship

As a Distinguished Professor at the University of Mysore's Department of Music, Dr. Manjunath has guided research scholars, shaped curriculum, and mentored the next generation. His son Sumanth Manjunath is himself a Gold Medallist, demonstrating that his influence on musical education extends even into his own household. The tradition he upholds will continue to produce musicians, scholars, and researchers for generations.

Youth Orchestra & Theatre

Through "Aaroha" (2022), Dr. Manjunath conceptualized and conducted a 20-member youth orchestra, creating new pathways for young musicians to engage with Carnatic music through the medium of theatre — breaking barriers between classical music and performing arts for a new generation of audiences.

Chapter Fourteen

Impact on the Field of Music & Art

Bridging East and West

The Los Angeles Times noted that Dr. Manjunath "has crossed over so many boundaries, that his style is purely personal." No higher compliment exists in world music: transcending tradition without abandoning it, creating something that belongs entirely to the artist. His Princeton workshop — where Western violinists spontaneously adopted Indian tradition the very next morning — remains one of the most compelling stories of musical cross-pollination in the modern era.

Expanding the Raga Vocabulary

Creating a new Raga is one of the rarest achievements in Carnatic music — it requires not just technical knowledge but a depth of musical understanding that can bring alive a new melodic personality. Dr. Manjunath has created multiple new ragas, including Yaduveera Manohari and Bharata. The latter, used in the Yoga Anthem, was verified by multiple music scholars as a genuinely new, melodically complete raga with the required bhaava (emotional essence).

Fusion Without Compromise

Dr. Manjunath has collaborated with jazz, western classical, Belgian contemporary, and Japanese musicians — not by diluting Carnatic music but by demonstrating its capacity for profound dialogue. His methodology of "sticking to the tradition with blending innovative styles" has become a model for how Indian classical music can engage with the world without losing its identity.

Taking Carnatic Music to Unexpected Spaces

From a royal palace in Milan to the prestigious Esplanade Theatre in Singapore, from the World Music Festival in Chicago to the Persian Academy of Culture in Tehran, Dr. Manjunath has placed Carnatic violin in contexts that most classical Indian musicians never imagined. His performances have consistently demonstrated that the grammar of a Raga transcends language, religion, and geography.

Music as Scholarship

His doctoral thesis on the violin from the University of Mysore is a scholarly contribution to the academic documentation of an instrument and tradition that has largely been transmitted orally. As a research supervisor himself, he has created a lineage not just of performers but of thinker-musicians who can articulate, document, and transmit this heritage.

Raga is not a mode as a westerner understands — it is a colour. Between orange and red there are thousands of colours, many waiting to be discovered by musicians yet to come.

— Dr. Mysore Manjunath, Mysuru Literature Festival 2021
